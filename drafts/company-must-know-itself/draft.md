# On the Same Growth Wavelength

*Annual reports and the stories companies and markets tell each other*

The [1969 Lowe's annual report](https://companiesmarketcap.com/annual-reports/282.ar.en.1969.pdf) is completely bananas.

The cover shows a psychedelic map of the world, shipping lines streaking across blue oceans, with **LOWE'S / A CYBERNETWORK** stamped across the top. Inside are computers, punch cards, teletype machines, trains, ships, highways and a family standing in front of their suburban home surrounded by every object Lowe's might conceivably sell them.

It looks less like the annual report of a regional hardware chain than a control manual for Spaceship Earth.

But the cybernetwork is not merely a groovy visual theme. It is a theory of the company. Suppliers of goods are “a merchandise network.” Suppliers of services are “a network of communication and transportation.” Stockholders are “a growth network.” Customers are “the reason-why network.” At the center are “Lowe's People: the control network.”

“Feed-back and feed-forward are required for success,” the report declares.

Lowe's was a $119 million retailer with 58 stores attempting to make sense of itself while information moved at accelerating speed. Fourteen years of growth had produced a web of customers, buyers, trucks, banks, manufacturers, store managers and IBM machines. The report does not simply enumerate them. It makes them part of the same thing.

The company was learning to see itself as a system.

## A constitution with a P&L attached

Annual reports are strange documents. Part audit, part advertisement. Part history, part forecast. A regulated disclosure wrapped in a discretionary self-portrait.

The numbers tell you what happened, or some version of it. The rest of the report tells you what kind of happening it was.

This is why the old reports are so much fun. They are unusually physical acts of corporate self-description. Photography entered annual reports in earnest around the middle of the twentieth century as companies learned that the report had a purpose beyond communicating financial information: it could [“sell the firm”](https://egrove.olemiss.edu/aah_notebook/vol13/iss1/30/) to the reader. The factories, laboratories, store openings and smiling employees were not decoration. They showed the reader what the numbers were made of.

The modern temptation is to treat all of this as spin layered on top of the real information. But the split is not so clean. Research into annual reports has found that the language of the narrative sections can contain [price-sensitive information](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0890838915300044) beyond the figures released alongside it. The narrative does not merely soften or exaggerate the accounts. It frames them.

Robert Shiller's idea of [narrative economics](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23075) is useful here. Stories do not sit outside the economy, commenting on it. They motivate spending and investment, connect activity to values, and circulate between people. A market narrative is not just a way of explaining the market. It is one of the things moving through it.

And an annual report is where a company tries to put its own narrative into circulation.

The phrase “market narrative” can make this sound cynical, as if the work is to find a good story and impose it on some inconvenient facts. Lowe's makes the idea stranger than that. The report was describing a cybernetwork, but it was also assembling one. It told suppliers, employees, customers and stockholders that their separate activities belonged to the same system. It gave management a language for expansion. It turned a regional chain into a node on a world map.

The report was not describing the company from the outside. It was helping the company become the thing it described.

Maybe an annual report is a kind of corporate constitution with a P&L attached. It says: these are the actors who count, these are the relationships between them, this is the past we share, and this is the future in which our actions will make sense.

## On the same growth wavelength

There is an especially revealing page in the Lowe's report devoted to stockholders. Management explains that its formal surveys and informal feedback show that “the large majority of our stockholders are on the same growth wavelength as management.”

What a phrase.

The market appears here not as a scoreboard outside the company but as part of its control system. Management broadcasts a growth story. Stockholders respond. Their response helps validate the strategy and fund more growth. Employees participate through the profit-sharing plan, which was itself the company's largest stockholder. The market is inside the company, and the company is trying to tune it.

This is close to what George Soros calls [reflexivity](https://www.georgesoros.com/2012/06/02/remarks_at_the_festival_of_economics_trento_italy/): the circular relationship between how participants understand a situation and how their decisions change the situation. In a company, the loop is everywhere. A market forecast justifies opening stores. The new stores alter the market. A corporate narrative attracts capital and talent. The capital and talent make the narrative more plausible. A measure becomes a target; the target reorganizes the work; the work moves the measure.

The company tells the market what it is. The market tells the company whether it believes it. Then the company acts on the answer.

The best annual reports make this loop visible because they have to join two kinds of sensemaking. They look backward to turn a year of scattered events into a coherent company, and forward to propose what that company might do next. In organizational theory, Karl Weick and his collaborators describe [sensemaking](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.1050.0133) as turning circumstances into a situation that can be comprehended in words and used as a springboard for action.

The annual report is one of the grand occasions for doing exactly that.

## The story survives the metaphor

Fifty-six years later, the cybernetwork is still there. Lowe's just no longer calls it one.

The [2025 annual report](https://corporate.lowes.com/sites/lowes-corp/files/2026-04/lowes-2025-annual-report.pdf) is organized around the “Total Home” strategy. It describes a Pro Extended Aisle connecting associates to suppliers' catalogs, an online marketplace that expands assortment without owning the inventory, loyalty systems containing more than 30 million members, an AI adviser for customers, and an AI companion for staff.

The psychedelic map has disappeared. The architecture has not.

In 1969, every sales ticket produced inventory-control information that flowed from the retail floor into IBM computers and back through the teletype network. In 2025, customer questions flow into an AI adviser, purchase behavior into a loyalty ecosystem, supplier inventories into an extended aisle. The annual report still has to turn all of this activity into one company with one direction.

Of course “Total Home” is no more a neutral description than “Cybernetwork” was. Each phrase foregrounds some possibilities and obscures others. Each makes a claim about the market the company believes it occupies. Each gives thousands of local decisions a larger plot.

This may be the most important function of a market narrative. It gives a company a way to recognize which business it is in before that answer is fully settled by the accounts.

## Standard status is not a story

Now, in the age of AI, every company is trying to become a cybernetwork again.

Enter standard status: an always-on context layer for the organization containing goals, decisions, dependencies, permissions and constraints. Almost every company I know is trying to build some version of it—a shared representation of the work that allows agents to act on token time without waiting for humans.

But standard status solves only one half of the problem.

Daily standups, weekly sprints, quarterly roadmaps and annual plans coordinate work. They also make the organization knowable to the people inside it. They are occasions to hear why a priority changed, test whether the strategy still matches reality, express disagreement and understand how your own judgment fits into the whole.

Meetings do more than describe a shared reality. They continually produce one.

Annual reports perform a slower, larger version of the same ritual. Someone has to decide which events belong in the story, which measures count, who the protagonists are, and what future makes the year legible. The report does not discover a pre-existing corporate intention and render it nicely. It is one of the places where that intention gets negotiated and made durable.

This is the part that will not fit neatly into `values.md` or `intentions.md`.

As companies become more legible to agents, they may become less legible to the people inside. Work might happen continuously, yet valuable work slows down because nobody is quite sure what matters, why it matters, or whether the company still wants the same thing. The official narrative might become perfectly machine-readable at exactly the moment it stops feeling collectively authored.

Worse, the narrative can disappear into the infrastructure. A metaphor chosen in a strategy meeting becomes an ontology. A temporary priority becomes a permanent field in the context layer. A contested interpretation becomes the canonical status agents act from. What was once visibly a story starts to look like the state of the world.

To coordinate agents, companies will have to formalize what they want. In doing so, they may discover that what looked like a single corporate intention was something people were continually making together.

The 1969 Lowe's report understood this better than its control-system language first suggests. It put people at the center, then admitted that the “company spirit” animating the network was impossible to define precisely. It had something to do with “electricity, of magnetism, of chemistry”—the human stuff the diagrams could point toward but not contain.

Perhaps the role of people in the cybernetwork of the future will not be to carry information through the system. Agents will be better at that. The human role may be to keep the company's market narrative alive: to argue with it, revise it, notice what it leaves out, and decide when the company is no longer the thing it said it was.

The company must know itself. But who gets to change its mind?
