# On the Same Growth Wavelength

*Annual reports, corporate moonshots and the stories companies and markets tell each other*

The [1969 Lowe's annual report](https://corporate.lowes.com/sites/lowes-corp/files/Annual%20reports%20and%20proxy%20statements/Lowes_AR_1969.pdf) is completely bananas.

The cover shows a psychedelic map of the world, shipping lines streaking across blue oceans, with **LOWE'S / A CYBERNETWORK** stamped across the top. Inside are computers, punch cards, teletype machines, trains, ships, highways and a family standing in front of their suburban home surrounded by every object Lowe's might conceivably sell them.

It looks less like the annual report of a regional hardware chain than a control manual for Spaceship Earth.

But the cybernetwork is not merely a groovy visual theme. It is a theory of the company. Suppliers, transport companies, customers and stockholders are each assigned their own network. At the center are the employees, designated as the system's controllers.

“Feed-back and feed-forward are required for success,” the report declares.

Lowe's was a $119 million retailer with 58 stores attempting to make sense of itself while information moved at accelerating speed. Fourteen years of growth had produced a web of customers, buyers, trucks, banks, manufacturers, store managers and IBM machines. The report does not simply enumerate them. It makes them part of the same thing.

The company was learning to see itself as a system.

Then, near the end of the report, the system leaves the planet. Earth hangs above a lunar horizon on a black page announcing the Lowe's cybernetwork as “Our Ultimate Achievement in Corporate Life-Style.”

It is easy to forget how much work the date is doing here. This was 1969, the year Apollo 11 landed on the Moon.

## Space-age management

A few months before the landing, former NASA administrator James Webb published [*Space Age Management: The Large-Scale Approach*](https://archive.org/details/spaceagemanageme0000webb). Webb believed Apollo's transferable invention was not the rocket but the organization: the ability to coordinate government agencies, universities, private companies and hundreds of thousands of people around a single destination.

The Moon landing was a spectacular event manufactured from mundane acts of management. Every wire had to be tested, every contractor synchronized, every meeting minuted. The mission depended on budgets, flowcharts, schedules, reports and chains of command extending all the way back to Earth.

In his paper [“Space age management”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1744935909337759), the organizational scholar Martin Parker describes the paradox this way: “organization produces excess, and excess requires organization.” The sublime achievement of Apollo depended on making almost everything beneath it boringly predictable. Adventure was the product of administration. Moon dust was made of paperwork.

Whether or not anyone at Lowe's had read Webb, they inhabited the same managerial atmosphere. Apollo had made an extraordinary proposition visible: a sufficiently complex network of people and machines could be coordinated into a single intentional system. The Moon landing was not merely a feat of engineering. It was systems thinking performed as public spectacle.

Lowe's borrowed that spectacle for retail. Apollo had Mission Control; Lowe's had IBM reports, teletypes, weekly meetings and quarterly bonuses. The annual report was both mission-control display and mission patch: a way of seeing the network and a story that made participating in it feel consequential.

To call 58 hardware stores a cybernetwork was not just description. It was an act of elevation.

## The 1,142 missing stores

Halfway through the report, the cybernetwork acquires a destination. Lowe's calculates that the United States needs 1,200 of its stores.

At the time, it has 58.

The figure is presented as market research, derived from the number of American households and their likely purchases. Management pauses to point out that just 500 stores, at the existing average sales volume, would place Lowe's among the country's billion-dollar retailers.

The arithmetic may have been analytical. Its function inside the report was prophetic. Lowe's was not simply accounting for 58 existing stores. It was teaching stockholders to see 1,142 missing ones.

Those imaginary stores were already surprisingly tangible. The ideal Lowe's required four acres beside a highway and railway, a 40,000-to-50,000-square-foot building, an air-conditioned showroom, warehouse, loading docks, lumber storage and a large paved parking lot. The future had a lot size.

In Knoxville, Lowe's had even torn down an existing store and replaced it on the same site while remaining open for business. Here was a rather perfect image of the company the report was proposing: continuously rebuilding itself without stopping the flow of sales.

This is the feed-forward half of the cybernetwork. The report does not merely represent the company to the market. It gives the network a future to construct.

## The company grows antennae

The feedback half was equally physical.

Lowe's regarded the 9,000 customers entering its stores each day as its most basic research system. Employees were supposed to listen. Information travelled through evening store meetings, weekly manager reports, monthly management meetings, telephones and teletypes. IBM reports made customer behavior visible from headquarters.

One example in the report concerns an exterior door. The computer could reveal that a particular style was selling poorly. Customers could explain that they wanted a cheaper door in a Mediterranean style. Neither could tell Lowe's where to obtain it. That required buyers listening to salespeople, visiting trade shows, reading catalogs and shopping competitors.

At grand openings, the feedback loop took the form of a carnival. There were refreshments, live music, children's rides and a “Heart's Desire” contest. Customers walked the displays and wrote down the product they most wanted to win. Usually it was a color television. Sometimes it was a percolator.

This is marvellous marketing texture. It is also a control system. The promotion manufactures desire, captures it as information and feeds it back into the company. Every transaction can be both a sale and a signal.

The market is not somewhere outside the cybernetwork. It is one of its senses.

## A constitution with a P&L attached

Annual reports are strange documents. Part audit, part advertisement. Part history, part forecast. A regulated disclosure wrapped in a discretionary self-portrait.

The numbers tell you what happened, or some version of it. The rest of the report tells you what kind of happening it was.

This is why the old reports are so much fun. They are unusually physical acts of corporate self-description. Photography entered annual reports in earnest around the middle of the twentieth century as companies learned that the report could do more than communicate financial information: it could [“sell the firm”](https://egrove.olemiss.edu/aah_notebook/vol13/iss1/30/) to the reader. The factories, laboratories, store openings and smiling employees were not decoration. They showed the reader what the numbers were made of.

The modern temptation is to treat all of this as spin layered over the real information. But the split is not so clean. Research into annual reports has found that the language of their narrative sections can contain [price-sensitive information](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0890838915300044) beyond the accompanying figures. The narrative does not merely soften or exaggerate the accounts. It frames them.

Robert Shiller's idea of [narrative economics](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23075) is useful here. Stories do not sit outside the economy, commenting on it. They motivate spending and investment, connect activity to values, and circulate between people. A market narrative is not just a way of explaining the market. It is one of the things moving through it.

The phrase can make this sound cynical, as though management's task is to find a sufficiently attractive story and impose it on some inconvenient facts. Lowe's makes the idea stranger than that. The report was describing a cybernetwork, but it was also assembling one. It told suppliers, employees, customers and stockholders that their separate activities belonged to the same system. It gave management a language for expansion. It turned a regional chain into a node on a world map.

The report was not describing the company from the outside. It was helping the company become the thing it described.

Maybe an annual report is a corporate constitution with a P&L attached. It says: these are the actors who count, these are the relationships between them, this is the past we share, and this is the future in which our actions will make sense.

Or perhaps it is an operating model told in public.

Fifty-six years later, the cybernetwork is still there. Lowe's just no longer calls it one. Its [2025 annual report](https://corporate.lowes.com/sites/lowes-corp/files/2026-04/lowes-2025-annual-report.pdf) is organized around the “Total Home” strategy. Supplier catalogs flow into an extended aisle, marketplace inventory appears without being owned, more than 30 million loyalty members generate data, and AI systems advise customers and staff.

The psychedelic map has disappeared. The architecture has not. Every era gives the corporate system a new name, and every name proposes a slightly different company.

## Resonance or echo

There is an especially revealing page in the Lowe's report devoted to stockholders. Management describes formal surveys, proxy tallies, annual-report questionnaires and informal feedback, then concludes that most stockholders share its appetite for growth.

The market appears here not as a scoreboard outside the company but as part of its control system. Management broadcasts a growth story. Stockholders respond. Their response helps validate the strategy and fund more growth. Employees participate through the profit-sharing plan, which was itself the company's largest stockholder. The market is inside the company, and the company is trying to tune it.

The tuning extended beyond investors. Salespeople received weekly commissions. Superior performance brought quarterly bonuses; company targets brought annual ones. Nine salesmen and managers had won new cars. A company newspaper was mailed to employees' homes, while wives received personal letters when their husbands performed particularly well. Fishing trips, dances and store parties cultivated the Lowe's spirit beyond working hours.

The cybernetwork reached into pay packets, garages and living rooms. Its narrative did not merely explain the system. It rewarded people for behaving like its components.

This is close to what George Soros calls [reflexivity](https://www.georgesoros.com/2012/06/02/remarks_at_the_festival_of_economics_trento_italy/): the circular relationship between how participants understand a situation and how their decisions change it. A market forecast justifies opening stores. The stores alter the market. A growth narrative attracts capital and talent. The capital and talent make the narrative more plausible.

The company tells the market what it is. The market tells the company whether it believes it. Then the company acts on the answer.

But Parker's account of space-age management reveals the tension hidden inside this success. Apollo could produce a radically surprising result only because the organization beneath it tried to eliminate surprise. Astronauts followed checklists. Contractors obeyed specifications. Mission Control made deviation visible. The adventure depended upon almost everybody behaving predictably.

Lowe's wanted the same kind of alignment. But a control system that achieves perfect agreement has a problem: it can no longer distinguish resonance from an echo.

A company needs a story coherent enough to organize action. It also needs a market capable of telling it that the story is wrong. Corporate fantasy is what happens when feed-forward overwhelms feedback.

A company can tell the market almost any story. The test is whether it has built a system capable of hearing the answer.

## The report comes with a reply card

The best detail in the 1969 report is saved for last.

The final page is a detachable, postage-paid questionnaire. It asks readers which feature they liked best, which seemed least useful, and what Lowe's should do differently. Then it asks whether they own Lowe's stock. If they do not, they can request financial mailings and investment information.

The distinction between feedback and marketing collapses on the page. A response might improve the next annual report. It might also produce the next shareholder.

This is not a postscript to the cybernetwork. It is part of the circuitry.

A corporation is too large to encounter all at once. No employee, customer, supplier or stockholder sees the whole thing. Each sees a store, a shipment, a paycheque, a dividend, a line on a screen. As a shared object, the company has to be continuously composed.

An annual report is a bid for that coherence. It gathers a year of local events and says: these belonged together. More than that, they happened because we are this kind of company, moving toward this kind of future.

But a market narrative is different from an ordinary brand story because it is exposed to consequence. The story travels outward and returns as behavior: a supplier extends terms, an employee stays, an investor pays a higher price. Or they do not. Every response changes the company the next report will have to explain.

The report gives the company a story. The market gives the story resistance. Between them, a temporary version of the company emerges.

On the last page, the cybernetwork adds one more component: the reader. Lowe's does not merely broadcast its grand narrative. It leaves blank lines for the market to write back.

The company must know itself. The reply card admits that it cannot do so alone.

The annual report did not merely describe a feedback loop. It came with one attached.
