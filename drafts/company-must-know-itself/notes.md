# On the Same Growth Wavelength

Working subtitle: *Annual reports and the stories companies and markets tell each other*

## Working thesis

An annual report is not merely a report on the company. It is a temporary settlement about what the company is, who it is for, and which future the market should believe.

A market narrative is an operating model told in public. It helps investors price the company, managers allocate against it, employees locate themselves inside it, and the company become more like the thing it claims to be.

The AI turn: companies are building shared context layers that make the organization legible to agents. The risk is that a collectively authored, revisable narrative hardens into canonical machine-readable status.

## Anchor details from the 1969 report

- Cover: psychedelic world map with shipping routes and “LOWE'S / A CYBERNETWORK.”
- 1969 scale: $119,052,889 in sales; 58 stores; 2,290,346 customers served.
- Suppliers of goods: “a merchandise network.”
- Suppliers of services: “a network of communication and transportation.”
- Lowe's people: “the control network.”
- Customers: “the reason-why network.”
- Stockholders: “a growth network.”
- The computers section describes sales-ticket information flowing from retail floors into inventory control, accounting, budgets, forecasts, sales analysis and financial reports.
- Exact quote: “Feed-back and feed-forward are required for success.”
- Exact phrase: stockholders are “on the same growth wavelength as management.”
- The employee profit-sharing plan was the company's largest stockholder.
- The report calls marketing an act of keeping the company's “antennae trained towards the market” and interpreting the signals.
- One spread declares Lowe's Cybernetwork “Our Ultimate Achievement in Corporate Life-Style.”
- The final human wrinkle: company spirit is “impossible to precisely define” and contains “electricity, of magnetism, of chemistry.”

## Tensions worth preserving

- Audit versus advertisement: the annual report is both evidence and persuasion.
- Description versus enactment: the report describes the company while helping bring that version of the company into being.
- Internal versus external: the market is not outside the company; its signals become managerial inputs, incentives and resource allocations.
- State versus narrative: an agent context layer can represent what is true without creating shared understanding of why it matters.
- Legibility versus authorship: the more canonical and machine-readable the corporate narrative becomes, the easier it is to forget that it was chosen and could be contested.
- Control versus spirit: the 1969 report uses systems language but still admits that the animating quality of the company cannot be precisely captured.
