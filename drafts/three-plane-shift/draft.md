At the end of the nineteenth century, a Swedish king gave the mathematicians of Europe a birthday problem.

King Oscar II was turning sixty. Gosta Mittag-Leffler, the ambitious founder of *Acta Mathematica*, persuaded him to sponsor an international prize competition. The most famous question was a version of the old celestial mechanics problem: given a system of bodies attracting each other according to Newton's law, could you describe their motion forever? Could you prove, in some rigorous way, that the heavens would keep their appointments?

Enter Henri Poincare, a polymath of the era, who in attempting to prove this theory actually proved something closer to the opposite: that even with only three bodies, you could not reliably predict their motion. It was the dawn of chaos theory and uncertainty.

Which feels like the right place to begin.

The three body problem is not simply "three things are complicated." It is the discovery that once three bodies are pulling on each other, the system no longer behaves like a tidy extension of the two-body case. The motion gets strange. Each body alters the path of the others. Prediction becomes impossible.

Working in modern corporate America in 2026, you can feel the chaos around you. Organizations, teams, and individuals have lost the ability to predict the future, and all of the scaffolding of "doing work" - annual planning, quarterly offsites, weekly status meetings - is increasingly feeling like theatre from an abandoned age.

And none more so than marketing teams, where AI is changing the trajectories of customers, channels, and process all at once. A classical three body problem of unpredictability.

## Act 1: The Three Body Problem Of Marketing

For marketing teams, the customer, the channel, and the process are interconnected. The image of them as bodies in space exerting gravity is accurate: they exert force on each other. And all of them are changing at an accelerating pace in 2026.

### Body #1: Customers

The old adage is that customer behavior changes slowly. That despite a technological revolution like the launch of the iPhone, it still took a decade for mobile usage to outstrip desktop. The idea being that customer needs and desires are somewhat stable.

The obvious thing to say is that AI adoption is dramatic. That is true, but too bland to be useful. [Pew reports](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/) that about four in ten U.S. adults use chatbots for information search, and 38% of employed adults use them for work tasks. [Google says](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/search-io-2026/) AI Mode has passed one billion monthly users, with queries more than doubling every quarter since launch.

It is not just adoption that is growing though. It is customers delegating research and decision-making to LLM models. Where search touched the middle part of the funnel - useful for exploring a purchase decision and a small amount of discovery - AI is capable of recommending novel brands and categories to you, staying with you through your buying journey, and ingesting your preferences and constraints to get you to the final purchase decision.

People are not just typing search queries into a different box. The shape of the ask is changing. Search queries used to be little fragments of intent: "best hotel Vancouver", "CRM pricing", "symptoms toddler cough", "how to fix dishwasher." Now the prompt is closer to a small brief. Compare these options. Make me a plan. Tell me what I am missing. Summarize this market. Pick the right one for my situation. Draft the email. Check the claim. Act on my behalf.

The customer is becoming slightly more agentic. Not in the breathless press-release sense. More practically: a growing number of people now expect to move through the world with a kind of cognitive sidecar. A model that reads before they read. A model that compares before they compare. A model that turns the open web into a memo before they ever reach your site.

Seeing this change all at once is hard, not least because so much AI influence is either zero-click or dark-click. But we know adoption is growing quickly, and we can feel the shape of the customer request changing.

This matters because a brand used to be interpreted mostly by people. Now it is interpreted by people plus the machine-shaped memory of the market.

So, yeah, customers are changing their behavior.

### Body #2: Channels

The rise of social was the last time marketing had to grapple with a new channel, and it was messy. But this time we are not only seeing a new channel emerge. We are seeing the old channels get rewritten underneath us.

**Search is being eaten by AI**

Search is the obvious one. Google has been the stable fact of digital marketing for something like fifteen years. The bargain was never pure, but it was legible. Publishers and brands allowed crawlers in. Google sent traffic back. Users searched. Results appeared. The click was the handshake.

AI search rewrites that bargain. AI Overviews and AI Mode change the contract between searcher, search engine, and brand. The answer is now assembled before the visit. The query can become a conversation. The search engine is not only an index but an interpreter.

This is why the crawler politics are so revealing. A [2026 BuzzStream analysis](https://www.buzzstream.com/blog/publishers-block-ai-study/) found that 79% of top news sites block at least one bot used for AI training, while 71% block at least one live search or retrieval bot. In the old web, almost nobody wanted to block search engines. Now publishers are making fine-grained decisions about training, retrieval, indexing, citation, and value exchange. That is not a small SEO footnote. That is the channel contract being renegotiated in public.

**Social is becoming spam**

Social is changing less because of what the big platforms are doing, and more because of what they are not doing. Facebook and Pinterest have been slow and lazy in dealing with their AI spam problems, leading to feeds of slop as far as the eye can see. But it is not just sloppy. It is spam. Motivated actors are actively spamming these platforms for profit, producing content at scale because they have discovered that synthetic media can still harvest attention, clicks, affiliate revenue, or platform payouts.

This is not a marginal problem. In certain categories, spam can feel like more than half the feed. More fake than real.

### Body #3: The Process

The third body is the way the marketing team itself works.

It almost goes without saying that the world of work is changing, but we should not become numb to it. We are so early. While "AI" has been around for a few years now, we are really just getting started.

Cloudflare noted that token usage was up 600% in its last earnings report. Coinbase has seen token usage explode in just the last few weeks: https://x.com/brian_armstrong/status/2070670644577280109

Codex usage outside of engineering teams has just exploded, but only in May/June 2026: https://x.com/daveholtz/status/2070147557314175072

So if the way work is created feels new, different, strange, you are not alone. The bottleneck is imagination now for how work works.

For marketing teams in particular, 2026 has seen a Cambrian explosion in image and video capabilities, with SeedDance, OpenAI image models, Gemini, and so much more.

But all of this brings a sense of uncertainty. You can recreate workflows in AI, but the old workflows are out. Customers and channels are changing, so what new workflows do we need? How should the team be structured?

This is where the three bodies start pulling hard on each other. If customers are changing and channels are changing, automating the old process is not obviously progress. You may simply be making yesterday's marketing faster. Or worse, making yesterday's marketing infinite.

When one body moves, you adjust. When two bodies move, you re-plan. When three bodies move, the system becomes difficult to predict from inside the system.

That is where marketing teams are now.

## Act 2: New Clocks, New Colonies

Organizations exist to reduce transaction costs. While coordination might feel like busywork, it is actually part of the foundation of why firms exist. So when new technologies come along that change industries, they also change the shape of organizations.

Sometimes they even change time itself.

Before standard time, noon was local. This was manageable when life moved at the speed of horses, church bells, shopfronts, and weather. But railroads made local time absurd. A train schedule cannot be different in every town. A missed minute in one place becomes a collision in another.

So on [November 18, 1883](https://americanhistory.si.edu/ontime/synchronizing/zones.html), U.S. railroads adopted standardized time zones. "Railroad time" came first as an operating necessity, then gradually became public time.

Railroads did not just move people faster. They forced a new coordination layer onto the world.

This is a useful way to think about AI inside companies. Not as magic. Not as a campaign gimmick. Not even as a productivity tool, exactly. As a coordination shock.

[Ronald Coase](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1468-0335.1937.tb00002.x) argued that firms exist, in part, because market coordination is costly. There are costs to finding people, negotiating, contracting, monitoring, enforcing, remembering. The organization is a way of lowering the cost of getting work done together.

AI has scrambled the equation here. Individual productivity has skyrocketed, but team productivity? That is less clear. That is why the term "slop cannon" has entered the lexicon.

But there is no inherent reason, at least that I can see, that AI might not be excellent at coordination. Shopify River, Alephic Intelligence, Claude Tag - companies are starting to integrate AI as a coworker in multiplayer environments. AI is also getting very, very good at things like meeting summaries, identifying action items, keeping track of projects, and updating status docs.

This is the WTFS problem Noah describes: what is the fucking status?

> "The basic notion is that much of the work that exists inside companies is people and software answering questions about the status of projects to their superiors. It's also one of the most frustrating parts of the job for both sides: bosses don't understand why it's so hard to answer a simple status inquiry, and middle managers wish their bosses knew how many people they have to wrangle just to answer what looks like an easy question."

The bottleneck moves from production to status.

With the backdrop of extreme layoffs in the tech sector, what is the shape of the modern marketing team? I certainly do not know, but here are three ideas.

### Sensemaking & Dynamic Capabilities

[Karl Weick's sensemaking](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.1050.0133) feels newly useful. In uncertain environments, organizations do not simply perceive the world and then act on it. They construct workable interpretations together. They make the situation legible enough to move. A good strategy frame is not a prediction machine. It is a shared noticing machine.

Marketing's three body problem is, in this sense, a sensemaking device. It gives the team language for why the chaos feels different. The problem is not owned by SEO. It is not owned by brand. It is not owned by marketing ops. It is not owned by the AI transformation team, God help us. The problem is the interaction between customers, channels, and the company's own capacity to act.

[David Teece's dynamic capabilities](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=3256) gives us the next move. In a shifting environment, advantage comes from the ability to sense, seize, and reconfigure. The firm has to keep rebuilding its own capabilities as the world changes. This is a more useful frame than "AI strategy," which too often means a slide deck, a vendor bakeoff, and three pilots that never quite become infrastructure.

Maybe the question for marketing leaders is not: what is our AI roadmap?

Maybe it is: what is our new clock?

What coordination layer lets the company make sense of faster work, stranger channels, and customers who are delegating parts of their cognition to machines?

### Of Termites & Tokens

In [Of Termites & Tokens](https://tomcritchlow.com/2026/06/08/termites-tokens/) I wrote about the "margin trap": using AI to make existing workflows cheaper instead of asking what new forms of organizational behavior become possible when small acts of cognition get cheap. The termite image is useful here because colonies do not coordinate by memo. They coordinate through traces in the environment. Pheromone trails. Local signals. Repeated interactions between actors and surroundings.

Organizations have their own pheromone trails: Slack threads, CRM fields, issue states, tags, transcripts, dashboards, source trails, approval markers, customer notes, analytics events, meeting summaries. In a low-throughput organization these are mostly residue. In an AI-accelerated organization they become infrastructure.

This is the management problem hiding under the tool conversation.

If everyone can make more work, the company needs better traces. Better status. Better provenance. Better memory. Better ways of knowing what is alive, what is dead, what is approved, what is speculative, what is useful, what is merely plausible.

Otherwise the organization becomes a factory for its own confusion.

## Act 3: Customers Are Lost. Give Them Almanacs.

There is another side to this. Your customers are lost too.

The sense of chaos and instability that we have outlined above points to a feeling in the world: customers are as lost as you are. What do customers want when the world becomes hard to predict?

They want almanacs. Maybe.

Take the Michelin guide. Before the Michelin Guide was a restaurant guide, it was a guide for early drivers. A tire company realized that if you wanted to sell more tires, you might first have to make the world more drivable. So the guide gave people maps, hotels, repair information, and eventually restaurants worth traveling for. They did not try to advertise tires. They expanded the imagination for what driving could be.

The best brands in the next few years may not be the ones with the flashiest AI features. They may be the ones that become field guides for a disoriented customer. Almanacs. Maps. Buyers' guides. Evidence libraries. Diagnostic tools. Decision aids. Promptable archives. Market weather reports. Translation layers. Things that help customers see, compare, decide, and act with more confidence.

What if a brand like Etsy educated customers on how to use ChatGPT for searching the vast Etsy library?

What if Target built a guide to using AI for interior design?

What if Walmart built a guide to meal planning using AI?

Of course AI is amazing at all these things. It is also terrible at all these things. I think for a brand to pull this off there has to be an honesty to the degree to which AI can be used in practical ways for practical tasks. Where is the WalmartBench eval showing how good every model is at finding products, searching the catalog, and guiding customers to the right things?

Maybe your customer needs an almanac.

This is, I think, the marketing opportunity inside the three body problem. If customers are changing, channels are changing, and companies are changing, then the market is not simply hungry for more messages. It is hungry for orientation.

Poincare went looking for stability and found stranger motion. The railroads went looking for speed and invented a new clock. Michelin went looking for tire demand and made a map of a new way to travel.

Marketing teams are stuck in a chaotic system. Prediction is gone. But you cannot let that lack of prediction take away your ability to act. And maybe helping your customers is the greatest marketing act of all.
