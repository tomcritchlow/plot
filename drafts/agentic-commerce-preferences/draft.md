# Models Of Desire

The old world problem was illegibility.

Markets could see transactions. They could see clicks, carts, search queries, loyalty cards, page views, returns. They could see the things that made it all the way through the interface and became behavior.

But they could not see the vague intention. The almost-purchase. The unresolved tradeoff. The book you would buy if only you knew its title. The hotel you would book if someone could collapse the tabs. The subscription you would cancel if the counterparty had not made cancellation feel like a small administrative war.

Most consumer preference never becomes demand. It stays illegible.

This is why the second-hand books example is so useful. Before the internet, an obscure out-of-print book might sit unnoticed in one town while the perfect buyer searched hopelessly in another. Once Amazon, AbeBooks, and Google made the book findable, the match could happen. But in Glenn and Sara Fisher Ellison's work on the [internet market for used books](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24197), better search does not simply make everything cheaper. It helps unusual objects find high-value buyers. More matches happen. Consumer surplus can rise. And prices can become more dispersed.

That is the counterintuitive hinge: better search can make markets more efficient and more expensive at the same time.

Higher prices are not always evidence that search failed. In highly differentiated markets, higher prices can be evidence that search got better.

This feels like the strange promise, and danger, of agentic commerce.

We keep imagining shopping agents as little procurement bots. Find the cheapest flight. Compare the blenders. Buy the groceries. But maybe the deeper shift is that agents make preferences legible earlier. They turn more of the messy interior of consumer desire into something structured enough to act on.

They do not merely reveal demand.

They model desire.

And models of desire are not neutral.

## The Preference Stack

A preference is not one thing. It is a stack.

At the surface are attributes: red, hardback, under $40, nearby, available tomorrow. Beneath that are consequences: saves me a trip, feels special, avoids Amazon, will not fall apart after two uses. Beneath that are values: independence, care, thrift, taste, environmental concern, loyalty to a certain kind of commerce.

Marketing researchers have had a version of this for a while. Jonathan Gutman's [means-end chain model](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224298204600207) links product attributes to consequences and then to personal values. Robert Woodruff's [customer value hierarchy](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=2571649) makes a similar move: customers evaluate attributes and consequences based on whether they help achieve higher-level goals and purposes.

This is exactly the shape an agent has to learn.

When I say "red," do I mean literal red? Or do I mean visible on stage, matches the old one, easy for my kid to spot, feels like a gift? When I say "independent store," do I mean a moral value, a trust heuristic, a neighborhood preference, an aesthetic preference, or a willingness to pay more to avoid Amazon?

The old web mostly saw the top of the stack. Search queries. Filters. Clicks. Carts. "Red shoes." "Hotel Lisbon." "Best blender."

Agentic commerce wants the whole stack.

## Preferences Are Not Found Objects

There is a tempting folk model of commerce where I already know what I want and the market's job is to help me find it. The preference exists first. The purchase comes later.

But consumer research has been poking holes in that for decades. In ["Constructive Consumer Choice Processes"](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/25/3/187/1795625), Bettman, Luce, and Payne argue that consumers often do not have stable, well-defined preferences sitting around waiting to be discovered. We construct preferences in context, using different strategies depending on the task, the emotional difficulty, the available options, and the effort required.

This matters for agents.

An agent is not just retrieving the truth of what I want. It is helping decide which version of my wanting becomes crisp enough to transact. It decides which comparisons to make salient. It decides whether "cheap" matters more than "quiet," whether "best reviewed" matters more than "close to the train," whether the weird outlier is worth considering.

To model desire is not simply to see what someone has chosen before.

It is to see what they might choose if the conflict were resolved for them.

## Gates And Weights

Some preferences are weights. Others are gates.

"I'd prefer a morning flight" is a weight. It can be traded against price, duration, or convenience.

"I will not fly Spirit" might be a gate.

"I prefer independent stores" sounds soft until the agent treats it as a hard constraint. "I need it to be red" sounds factual until the agent learns red is negotiable if the deeper value is "easy to see in a crowded room."

Decision researchers distinguish between compensatory and non-compensatory choice strategies. In a compensatory model, a weakness on one attribute can be offset by strength on another. In a non-compensatory model, an option can be eliminated because it fails one important condition. The [Nielsen Norman Group has a useful explainer](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/compensatory-noncompensatory-decisions/) on how these show up in user experience, and the academic literature has long studied lexicographic and elimination-style rules.

This is not a minor UX detail. It is the grammar of desire.

An agent has to know whether a preference is a filter, a tradeoff, a value, a story, or a self-description. And the moment it knows that, the market may want to know too.

## Deferred Demand

This is where Tversky and Shafir's ["Choice under Conflict"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00047.x) becomes interesting. They show that when choices involve more conflict, people are more likely to delay, seek new alternatives, or fall back to defaults. Sometimes adding better options makes people less likely to choose, because the tradeoffs become harder to resolve.

This is the internet shopping condition.

Too many hotels. Too many tabs. Too many tradeoffs. Too much uncertainty around fit. A lot of consumer intent is not missing. It is suspended.

The abandoned cart is not always weak demand. Sometimes it is unresolved demand.

Agentic commerce may create markets by resolving these deferred preferences: the trip not booked, the gift not bought, the shoes not replaced, the credit card not renegotiated, the subscription not cancelled. It turns "I should probably..." into action.

That can be genuinely useful. It can also be a new extraction frontier.

Because once deferred demand becomes legible, it can be priced.

## The Legibility Trap

If my agent helps construct and resolve my preferences, who else gets to learn from that?

This is the rent question hiding under the convenience story. Better search helps me find the perfect thing. But it also helps the market discover that I am the perfect buyer. The used-book seller can wait for me. The hotel can raise prices because the recommendation engine keeps delivering its ideal guests.

Aaron Kaye's work on [personalized recommendations](https://www.aaron-kaye.com/research) pushes this directly: personalization can improve match quality while weakening price competition. The consumer finds a better fit. The seller faces a better-matched, less price-sensitive consumer. The market becomes more efficient, but also more capable of extracting willingness to pay.

So agentic commerce may not flatten markets. It may make them more jagged.

More long-tail abundance. More perfect matches. More welfare in some places. More price discrimination in others. Not one market-clearing price, but many little markets organized around increasingly legible desire.

The provocation is this: agentic commerce does not just give consumers better agents. It gives markets better-described consumers.

## Agents Or Rights?

Frank Chimero has the right cold bucket of water here. In ["Beyond the Machine Extras"](https://frankchimero.com/blog/2025/beyond-the-machine-extras/), he points out that many things we want agents to do are hard because companies made them hard: cancelling subscriptions, renegotiating rates, syncing siloed data, booking travel at the best cost. His summary is brutal and useful: "We don't need an agent, we need better legislation."

Yes.

A lot of agentic commerce is consumer protection cosplay. We ask for an agent when what we really want is a right: portability, cancellation, interoperability, transparent pricing, no dark patterns.

But if agents become the way preferences are modeled and entered into markets, then regulation has to move upstream. Not just "can my agent click cancel?" but "what rights attach to my model of desire?"

Can it be portable? Can it be selectively disclosed? Can it hide my willingness to pay? Can it preserve ambiguity as a bargaining strategy? Can it refuse to resolve certain choices? Can sellers refuse consumer agents? Can platforms force their own agents into the loop?

The grocery demos are boring because groceries are too simple.

The real terrain is adversarial preference construction.

## What Opens Up

Maybe the next consumer product is not a shopping agent but a preference wallet: a place where tastes, constraints, substitutions, unresolved intentions, and bargaining posture live under user control.

Maybe brands compete not just for attention, but for inclusion in the agent's constructed choice set.

Maybe the new SEO is not "be found by Google," but "be admitted into the buyer's model of desire."

Maybe agents monetize deferral: all the almost-bought, should-have-switched, meant-to-book, too-hard-to-decide moments that never became transactions.

And maybe preference privacy becomes more important than data privacy. The sensitive thing is not only what I bought. It is what I nearly bought, what I might buy, what I can be made to want, and which conflicts cause me to give up.

The old web revealed preferences by watching behavior.

The agentic web may construct preferences by resolving conflict.

The first made us trackable. The second might make us negotiable.

Or simply more perfectly priced.
